\begin{abstract}
For $4/3\leq p<2$, we prove that the explicit entire function
$F_p(z)=\sum_{j\geq1}z^{\lfloor j^p\rfloor}/\sqrt{\lfloor j^p\rfloor!}$
has order two, type $1/2$, and a zero of every sufficiently high derivative in each nonempty open set. For $p>4/3$, a discrete concavity argument identifies all derivative zeros on fixed annuli with small perturbations of explicit circular grids. It gives a sharp covering scale, eventual simplicity away from the origin, and a limiting zero measure. At $p=4/3$, a compactness argument for bilateral Gaussian series gives the sharp annular covering scale $n^{-1/4}$ without discarding the phases introduced by integer parts. These are deterministic constructions for the cofinite formulation of Erd\H{o}s Problem~906; no first-solution claim is made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Statements and context}\label{sec:intro}
For a nonzero entire function $g$, let $\Zer(g)$ be its zero set. The property under consideration is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cofinite}
 \forall U\subset\C\text{ nonempty open},\quad
 \exists N(U)\quad\forall n\geq N(U):\quad
 \Zer(f^{(n)})\cap U\ne\varnothing.
\end{equation}
It is equivalent to density of $\bigcup_k\Zer(f^{(n_k)})$ for every strictly increasing infinite sequence $(n_k)$ of nonnegative integers. Indeed, an infinite set of exceptional orders for one open set gives a sequence violating density; conversely, an increasing infinite sequence eventually exceeds any fixed threshold. We require $f$ to be transcendental, since polynomials make the question trivial.

This is the question in Erd\H{o}s \cite[p.~72, IV.1(i)]{Erdos82}, now numbered 906. The adjacent interpolation question, associated with Problem~229, concerns prescribed discrete sets and derivative orders selected during the construction. Barth and Schneider \cite[Theorem~1]{BS72} settle that interpolation problem. It does not itself provide the cofinite quantifier in \eqref{eq:cofinite}. MacLane's derivative universality theorem \cite{MacLane52} is another related density result.

Hou \cite[Theorem~1.2]{Hou26} proves \eqref{eq:cofinite} by a bounded random Fock series, with a finite quadratic exponential growth bound. His public repository also contains a Lean formalization; I have not rerun it. His introduction records earlier probabilistic proposals by Almeida and Chojecki. I am interested here in a different feature: an explicit sparse series whose derivative zeros can be tracked rather precisely. I do not claim the existence statement, or order-two finite-type existence, as new.

Fix $p\in[4/3,2)$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:series}
 \nu_j=\lfloor j^p\rfloor,\quad q_j=\nu_{j+1}-\nu_j,\quad
 \beta=1-\frac1p,\qquad F_p(z)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{z^{\nu_j}}{\sqrt{\nu_j!}}.
\end{equation}
For $0<a<b$, put $\Aab=\{z:a\leq |z|\leq b\}$ and
$h_n(E)=\sup_{w\in E}\dist(w,\Zer(F_p^{(n)}))$.
All constants below may depend on fixed radii and on $p$, but not on $n$ or on points in the indicated sets.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The function $F_p$ is transcendental entire, satisfies \eqref{eq:cofinite}, and has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:growth}
 \log M_{F_p}(r)=\tfrac12r^2+O_p(\log r)\qquad(r\to\infty).
\end{equation}
In particular, its order is exactly two and its type is $1/2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If $4/3<p<2$, then for every $R>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cover}
 h_n(\{|z|\leq R\})\leq C_{R,p}
       \bigl(n^{\beta-1/2}+n^{-\beta}\bigr)
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $n$. On every fixed annulus $\Aab$, this implies, and can be sharpened to,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sharp}
 c_{a,b,p}n^{\beta-1/2}\leq h_n(\Aab)
       \leq C_{a,b,p}n^{\beta-1/2}.
\end{equation}
\item If $p=4/3$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:criticalcover}
 c_{a,b}n^{-1/4}\leq h_n(\Aab)\leq C_{a,b}n^{-1/4}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $n$.
\end{enumerate}
The upper-bound proof in \eqref{eq:criticalcover} is a compactness argument; it does not give an explicit numerical value for $C_{a,b}$.
\end{theorem}

For $\nu_j\geq n$, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
 m_j=\nu_j-n,\quad A_j=\frac{\sqrt{\nu_j!}}{m_j!},\quad
 T_j(z)=A_jz^{m_j},\quad
 \rho_j=\left(\frac{A_j}{A_{j+1}}\right)^{1/q_j}.
\end{equation}
Dependence on $n$ is suppressed in these four quantities. Define the model centers
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centers}
 z_{j,\ell}=\rho_j\exp\!\left(\frac{(2\ell+1)\pi i}{q_j}\right),
 \qquad 0\leq\ell<q_j.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:geometry}
Suppose $4/3<p<2$. Fix $0<a<b$. There are $c>0$ and a threshold such that, with
$\eta_n=\exp(-c n^{2\beta-1/2})$, the disks
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disks}
 D\left(z_{j,\ell},\frac{\rho_j\eta_n}{q_j}\right),
 \qquad a/2\leq\rho_j\leq2b,
\end{equation}
are pairwise disjoint and each contains exactly one zero of $F_p^{(n)}$, counted with multiplicity. Every zero in $\Aab$ belongs to one of these disks. Thus every zero on $\Aab$ is eventually simple.

Let $S=\{re^{i\theta}:a<r<b,\ \alpha<\theta<\alpha+\omega\}$, where $0<\omega\leq2\pi$, with angles interpreted on the circle. If $N_n(S)$ counts zeros with multiplicity, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
 N_n(S)=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}(b-a)\sqrt n
       +O_{a,b,p}\bigl(n^\beta+n^{1/2-\beta}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Consequently, on $\C\setminus\{0\}$, in the sense of integration against continuous compactly supported functions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:measure}
 \frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{F_p^{(n)}(\zeta)=0}\!\delta_\zeta
       \ \longrightarrow\ \frac{1}{2\pi|z|}\dd A(z).
\end{equation}
Zeros in the sum are repeated according to multiplicity.
\end{theorem}

The support in \eqref{eq:series} is not a Hadamard gap sequence: $\nu_{j+1}/\nu_j\to1$. For this construction the useful scale is instead the gap compared with the differentiated saddle width. For $p>4/3$ it is
$n^{1/4}\ll q_j\ll n^{1/2}$ near $\nu_j=n+O(\sqrt n)$.
Gethner's work on final sets \cite{Gethner85} and Offord's work on lacunary entire functions \cite{Offord93} provide earlier context. A final-set assertion formulated using infinitely many orders does not alone imply \eqref{eq:cofinite}. I have only been able to compare the present argument with Offord's publisher abstract, which concerns angular zero distribution and growth away from small zero neighborhoods; a full comparison with his hypotheses remains open. Accordingly I make no priority claim for the sparse construction.

\section{Analytic preliminaries and the crossing radii}\label{sec:weights}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gaps}
The integers $\nu_j$ strictly increase and
\[
 q_j=pj^{p-1}(1+o(1))=p\nu_j^\beta(1+o(1)).
\]
Writing $Q=n^\beta$, there are constants $\gamma,\Gamma>0$ such that, for large $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gapbounds}
 q_h\geq\gamma Q\quad(\nu_h\geq n),\qquad
 q_h\leq\Gamma Q\quad(n\leq\nu_h\leq n+B\sqrt n)
\end{equation}
for each fixed $B$. Moreover $q_h/(pQ)\to1$ uniformly in the latter range. The first active exponent satisfies $m_{j_0}=O(Q)$, where $j_0=\min\{j:\nu_j\geq n\}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The mean value theorem gives
$q_j=p\xi_j^{p-1}+O(1)$ with $j<\xi_j<j+1$, and the floor error has modulus at most one. Since $(j+1)^p-j^p>1$, the exponents strictly increase. For active indices $j\geq n^{1/p}$, this gives, for example, $\gamma=p/2$ eventually. In a fixed saddle range $j/n^{1/p}\to1$ uniformly, giving the upper bound and the uniform equivalent. The exponent immediately preceding $\nu_{j_0}$ lies below $n$, so the same gap estimate bounds $m_{j_0}$. No monotonicity of the integer gaps is assumed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:analytic}
The series \eqref{eq:series} is entire, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative}
 F_p^{(n)}(z)=\sum_{j\geq j_0}A_jz^{m_j}.
\end{equation}
It satisfies \eqref{eq:growth}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For every $\varepsilon>0$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CS}
 \sum_{k\geq0}\frac{r^k}{\sqrt{k!}}
 \leq\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}
       \exp\!\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}r^2\right).
\end{equation}
This proves normal convergence. Cauchy's integral formula on a slightly larger circle justifies termwise differentiation to every fixed order and gives \eqref{eq:derivative}. Infinitely many Taylor coefficients are nonzero, so the function is transcendental.

Taking $\varepsilon=r^{-2}$ in \eqref{eq:CS} yields the upper bound in \eqref{eq:growth}. For the lower bound choose $k=\nu_j\leq r^2<\nu_{j+1}$. Then $r^2-k=O_p(r^{2\beta})=o(r)$. The elementary integral bounds for the increasing function $\log t$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factorial}
 k\log k-k+1\leq\log(k!)\leq k\log k-k+1+\log k.
\end{equation}
Thus
\[
 \log\frac{r^k}{\sqrt{k!}}
 =\tfrac12\{k+k\log(r^2/k)\}+O(\log k)
 =\tfrac12r^2+O_p(\log r).
\]
The last step follows by Taylor expansion in $(r^2-k)/r^2$, with error $O((r^2-k)^2/r^2)=o(1)$. Positivity at $z=r$ supplies the required lower bound. Under the definitions
$\operatorname{ord}f=\limsup\log\log M_f(r)/\log r$ and
$\operatorname{type}_2f=\limsup\log M_f(r)/r^2$, the order and type follow.
\end{proof}

The term ``Fock'' refers here to the normalization $1/\sqrt{k!}$; the coefficient sequence of $F_p$ is not square summable, so no membership in the standard Fock Hilbert space is asserted.

For integers $k\geq n$ and $r>0$ set
\[
 \Phi_{n,r}(k)=\tfrac12\log(k!)-\log((k-n)!)+(k-n)\log r,
 \qquad d_{n,r}(k)=\Phi_{n,r}(k+1)-\Phi_{n,r}(k).
\]
\begin{lemma}[Discrete curvature]\label{lem:curvature}
Writing $m=k-n$, one has
\begin{align}
 d_{n,r}(k)&=\log r+\tfrac12\log(n+m+1)-\log(m+1),\label{eq:difference}\\
 d_{n,r}(k)-d_{n,r}(k+1)
 &=\log\left(1+\frac1{m+1}\right)
       -\tfrac12\log\left(1+\frac1{n+m+1}\right)>0.\label{eq:curvature}
\end{align}
For any $B>0$, uniformly for $0\leq m\leq B\sqrt n$, the difference in \eqref{eq:curvature} is at least $c_B/\sqrt n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, where one may take $c_B=1/(4(B+1))$. If $m/\sqrt n\to r\in[a,b]$, it equals $(1+o(1))/(r\sqrt n)$ uniformly.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The formulas follow by cancellation of factorials. Positivity follows since $n+m+1\geq m+1$. The inequalities $x/(1+x)\leq\log(1+x)\leq x$ give the lower bound
$1/(m+2)-1/(2(n+m+1))$, which is at least $c_B/\sqrt n$ eventually. Expansion of the two logarithms proves the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Radial grid]\label{lem:radii}
The radii $\rho_j$ strictly increase to infinity for each fixed $n$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhoexact}
 \log\rho_j=\frac1{q_j}\sum_{s=1}^{q_j}
       \left\{\log(m_j+s)-\tfrac12\log(n+m_j+s)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Uniformly for $0\leq m_j\leq B\sqrt n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhoapprox}
 \rho_j=\frac{m_j}{\sqrt n}+O_{B,p}(Q/\sqrt n).
\end{equation}
Conversely, bounded $\rho_j$ imply $m_j=O(\sqrt n)$. On fixed compact annuli, consecutive crossing radii have spacings
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radialspacing}
 c_{a,b,p}\frac{Q}{\sqrt n}\leq\rho_{j+1}-\rho_j
       \leq C_{a,b,p}\frac{Q}{\sqrt n}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:rhoexact} follows from \eqref{eq:weights}. Put
$g_n(t)=\log(t+1)-\tfrac12\log(n+t+1)$ for $t\geq0$ and
$f_n(t)=\exp(g_n(t))$. Both increase strictly. Formula \eqref{eq:rhoexact} averages $g_n$ over successive disjoint integer blocks, proving strict ordering, divergence, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhosqueeze}
 f_n(m_j)\leq\rho_j\leq f_n(m_j+q_j-1).
\end{equation}
For $t=O(\sqrt n)$, $f_n(t)=t/\sqrt n+O(n^{-1/2})$, giving \eqref{eq:rhoapprox}, including $m_j=0$. The lower bound in \eqref{eq:rhosqueeze}, squared, shows that $\rho_j\leq b$ forces $(m_j+1)^2\leq b^2(n+m_j+1)$, hence $m_j=O_b(\sqrt n)$.

On the relevant blocks, Lemma~\ref{lem:curvature} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logspacing}
 \log\rho_{j+1}-\log\rho_j
 \geq\frac{c_B(q_j+q_{j+1})}{2\sqrt n}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the mean difference of the indices in the two consecutive blocks is $(q_j+q_{j+1})/2$; apply the curvature bound to every pair and average. On an annulus the blocks also satisfy $m_j\asymp\sqrt n$, where the reverse curvature bound is $C/\sqrt n$. This proves \eqref{eq:radialspacing}. Enlarging the fixed saddle range slightly includes both blocks throughout.
\end{proof}

\section{The two-term regime}\label{sec:twoterm}
Assume throughout this section that $\beta>1/4$. We give the infinite-tail estimate explicitly.

\begin{lemma}[Stable tail bound]\label{lem:tail}
Fix $B>0$ and $L>0$. There is $c>0$ such that, with
$E_n=cQ^2/\sqrt n\to\infty$, every active $j$ with $m_j\leq B\sqrt n$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
 \frac{\sum_{h\ne j,j+1}|T_h(z)|}{|T_j(z)|}
 \leq(1+\ee^L)\frac{\ee^{-E_n}}{1-\ee^{-E_n}}
 \quad\text{if}\quad |q_j\log(|z|/\rho_j)|\leq L.
\end{equation}
The threshold may depend on $B,L,p$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At $r=\rho_j$, the $q=q_j$ numbers $d_{n,r}(\nu_j),\ldots,d_{n,r}(\nu_j+q-1)$ have sum zero. Each consecutive drop is at least $c_0/\sqrt n$, where $c_0=1/(4(B+2))$ is valid after increasing the threshold. Thus their first and last members are respectively at least
$c_0(q-1)/(2\sqrt n)$ and at most its negative. For $q\geq2$, replace this magnitude by $c_0q/(4\sqrt n)$.

Changing the radius adds $\log(|z|/\rho_j)$ to every full difference. Since
$L/q=o(q/\sqrt n)$, the first and last values retain the corresponding signs with magnitude at least $c_0q/(8\sqrt n)$. Global monotonicity of $d_{n,r}$ now controls \emph{all} preceding and following blocks, even outside the saddle range. Every active gap is at least $\gamma Q$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:gaps}; hence each sparse step away from the pair loses at least
$E_n=c_0\gamma^2Q^2/(8\sqrt n)$ in logarithmic modulus.

The left tail is at most $|T_j|\sum_{s\geq1}\ee^{-sE_n}$. The right tail is at most $|T_{j+1}|\sum_{s\geq1}\ee^{-sE_n}$, and $|T_{j+1}|/|T_j|\leq\ee^L$. Summing these geometric series proves \eqref{eq:tail}; the actual left tail may terminate earlier.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rouch\'e transfer]\label{prop:rouche}
Uniformly for $0\leq m_j\leq B\sqrt n$, every disk
$D(z_{j,\ell},\rho_j\eta_n/q_j)$ contains exactly one zero, counted with multiplicity, where $\eta_n=\exp(-E_n/2)$ and $E_n$ is chosen in Lemma~\ref{lem:tail} with $L=2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $H_j=T_j+T_{j+1}$. At $z=z_{j,\ell}(1+u)$,
\[
 \frac{H_j(z)}{T_j(z)}=1-(1+u)^{q_j}.
\]
On $|u|=\eta_n/q_j$, one has $|q_j\log|1+u||\leq2\eta_n\leq2$. The binomial theorem yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modelbound}
 |1-(1+u)^{q_j}|\geq1+2\eta_n-\ee^{\eta_n}\geq\eta_n/2
\end{equation}
eventually. The last inequality follows from
$\ee^t-1-t\leq t^2$ for $0\leq t\leq1/4$, directly from its series.
The right side of \eqref{eq:tail} is $O(\ee^{-E_n})<\eta_n/2$. Thus
$|F_p^{(n)}-H_j|<|H_j|$ on the entire boundary.

The model has the zero $0$ of multiplicity $m_j$, if $m_j>0$, and exactly the $q_j$ simple nonzero zeros \eqref{eq:centers}. The disk excludes $0$. Distinct nonzero model zeros have distance at least $2\rho_j\sin(\pi/q_j)\geq4\rho_j/q_j$ for $q_j\geq2$, so it contains exactly one. Rouch\'e's theorem proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Exclusion of additional zeros]\label{prop:exclusion}
For every fixed annulus, the disks in Theorem~\ref{thm:geometry}, with a sufficiently small fixed choice of $c$, are disjoint and contain all its zeros for large $n$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The same-circle separation was proved above. Between circles, \eqref{eq:radialspacing} gives a separation of order $Q/\sqrt n$, whereas disk radii are $O(\eta_n/Q)$. Their ratio tends to zero, proving disjointness.

Fix $r\in[a,b]$ and choose a largest term $|T_k|$ on $|z|=r$. Such a term exists since the summands tend to zero. The normalized block slopes
\[
 \frac1{q_j}\log\frac{|T_{j+1}|}{|T_j|}=\log r-\log\rho_j
\]
strictly decrease. They show that the moduli increase up to a maximum and then decrease. The first crossing tends to zero. From \eqref{eq:rhosqueeze}, the maximizing index and its fixed number of neighbors satisfy $m_k\asymp\sqrt n$ and $q_k\asymp Q$, uniformly in $r$.

Write $u=|T_{k-1}|/|T_k|\leq1$ and $v=|T_{k+1}|/|T_k|\leq1$. The crossing separation gives
\[
 \frac{\log u}{q_{k-1}}+\frac{\log v}{q_k}
 =\log\rho_{k-1}-\log\rho_k\leq-cQ/\sqrt n.
\]
Both $u$ and $v$ cannot be at least $1/4$, since that would bound the left side below by $-C/Q$, while $Q^2/\sqrt n\to\infty$.

All terms beyond these two neighbors have total modulus $O(\ee^{-c'Q^2/\sqrt n})|T_k|$. To verify this last statement, on the block immediately preceding $k-1$ its normalized slope exceeds that of block $k-1$ by at least $cQ/\sqrt n$, by \eqref{eq:logspacing}. It is therefore positive by this amount. Earlier normalized slopes are larger still. Multiply by the global lower gap bound and sum a geometric series. The right side is identical with reversed signs. This argument uses local curvature only at the two initial blocks and global monotonicity thereafter.

If $u,v<1/4$, the largest term exceeds the sum of all others, so there is no zero. Otherwise select the neighboring pair $j,j+1$ containing a neighbor of relative size at least $1/4$. It has
$|q_j\log(r/\rho_j)|\leq\log4<2$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:tail} bounds its remainder by $R_n|T_j|$, with $R_n=O(\ee^{-E_n})$ after choosing a common smaller $E_n=cQ^2/\sqrt n$.

At a zero choose the nearest model angle and write
$z=z_{j,\ell}\ee^{v_0/q_j}$, where
$|\Rea v_0|\leq\log4$ and $|\Im v_0|\leq\pi$.
For $v_0=x+iy$ in this rectangle,
\[
 |1-\ee^{v_0}|^2=(\ee^x-1)^2+4\ee^x\sin^2(y/2)
       \geq\tfrac1{16}|v_0|^2.
\]
Here $|\ee^x-1|\geq |x|/4$ and $|\sin(y/2)|\geq|y|/\pi$ suffice. At the zero, $|1-\ee^{v_0}|\leq R_n$, hence $|v_0|\leq4R_n$ and
$|z-z_{j,\ell}|\leq8\rho_jR_n/q_j<\rho_j\eta_n/q_j$ eventually. Also $\rho_j=r\exp(O(Q^{-1}))\in[a/2,2b]$. This proves the exclusion assertion.
\end{proof}

\section{Covering, counting and the origin}\label{sec:count}
Let $|w|=r\leq R$. If $n+r\sqrt n\geq\nu_{j_0}$, choose $j$ with
$\nu_j\leq n+r\sqrt n<\nu_{j+1}$; otherwise take $j=j_0$. Lemmas~\ref{lem:gaps} and \ref{lem:radii} give
\[
 |\rho_j-r|\leq C Q/\sqrt n,
 \qquad \rho_j\leq R+o(1),\qquad q_j\asymp Q.
\]
For $w\ne0$, a nearest model angle has angular error at most $\pi/q_j$. Proposition~\ref{prop:rouche} therefore supplies a true zero within
$C(Q/\sqrt n+Q^{-1})$ of $w$. For $w=0$, use any center of the first pair; its radius is $O(Q/\sqrt n)$. This proves \eqref{eq:cover} on the whole disk, without extending an annular estimate across the origin.

For the lower bound in \eqref{eq:sharp}, choose two consecutive crossing radii in a fixed smaller annulus inside $\Aab$. Such radii exist for all large $n$ by \eqref{eq:rhoapprox}. Their separation is at least $cQ/\sqrt n$. A point at the midpoint radius is at least half this radial separation from all model circles. Proposition~\ref{prop:exclusion} and the exponentially small disk radii preserve a lower bound $c'Q/\sqrt n$ for the distance to true zeros. Zeros outside a fixed enlargement of the annulus are a fixed positive distance away. This proves the sharp scale.

To count, let $J_n(a,b)=\{j:a<\rho_j<b\}$. It is an interval of consecutive indices, and \eqref{eq:rhoapprox} implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:telescoping}
 \sum_{j\in J_n(a,b)}q_j=(b-a)\sqrt n+O(Q),
 \qquad \#J_n(a,b)=O(\sqrt n/Q).
\end{equation}
For the first assertion, the sum telescopes to the difference of its endpoint support exponents; the first and last $m_j$ differ from $a\sqrt n$ and $b\sqrt n$ by $O(Q)$, using the same statement for their adjacent radii. Each circle contributes $\omega q_j/(2\pi)+O(1)$ model centers to an arc of length $\omega$. Equations \eqref{eq:telescoping} give \eqref{eq:count} for centers.

Passing to true zeros changes this count by at most $O(Q+\sqrt n/Q)$. At either radial boundary at most one model circle is within the maximal disk radius for large $n$, by \eqref{eq:radialspacing}; each carries $O(Q)$ centers. At either angular boundary each relevant circle has only $O(1)$ affected centers, since the angular displacement is $O(\eta_n/Q)$. This also handles centers or zeros lying exactly on a boundary, and proves \eqref{eq:count}. Approximation of a continuous compactly supported function by step functions on finitely many annular sectors proves \eqref{eq:measure}; \eqref{eq:count} on a containing annulus uniformly bounds the masses needed for this approximation.

At the origin the exact multiplicity is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:origin}
 \operatorname{mult}_0 F_p^{(n)}=\min_{\nu_j\geq n}(\nu_j-n)=m_{j_0},
\end{equation}
where multiplicity zero means a nonzero value. In particular, taking $n=\nu_j+1$ gives multiplicity $q_j-1\to\infty$. Thus a claim of eventual simplicity on the whole plane would be false. This does not affect the disk covering theorem or the annular statements.

\section{The critical gap exponent}\label{sec:critical}
We now put $p=4/3$, so $\beta=1/4$. The two-term remainder need not tend to zero on a fixed annulus. Instead, a rescaled derivative has subsequential limits among bilateral Gaussian series with arbitrary phases.

\begin{lemma}[Bilateral Gaussian series have zeros]\label{lem:gaussian}
Let $\kappa>0$, $t\in\mathbb R$, and $|c_s|=1$ for every $s\in\Z$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussian}
 G(v)=\sum_{s\in\Z}c_s\exp(ts-\kappa s^2/2)\ee^{sv}
\end{equation}
is a nonzero entire function and has a zero in $\C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Gaussian decay proves local uniform convergence and a bound
$|G(v)|\leq\exp(C(1+|v|^2))$. The function is $2\pi i$-periodic. Its Fourier coefficients on the imaginary axis are precisely the nonzero numbers $c_s\exp(ts-\kappa s^2/2)$; absolute convergence justifies integration, and in particular $G\not\equiv0$.

If $G$ had no zeros, it would admit an entire logarithm $h$, with $\Rea h(v)\leq C(1+|v|^2)$. We recall a short proof that $h$ is quadratic. Write $h(v)=\sum a_kv^k$, $u=\Rea h$, and $M_R=\max_{|v|=R}u(v)$. For $k\geq1$, the Fourier formula and the mean-value property give
\[
 |a_k|R^k
 =\left|\frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}u(R\ee^{i\theta})\ee^{-ik\theta}\dd\theta\right|
 \leq 2(M_R-u(0))=O(1+R^2).
\]
Thus $a_k=0$ for $k\geq3$. Periodicity implies
$h(v+2\pi i)-h(v)\in2\pi i\Z$, so this polynomial difference is constant. The quadratic coefficient vanishes and the linear coefficient is an integer. Consequently $G(v)=C_0\ee^{kv}$ for some $k\in\Z$, contradicting the nonzero Fourier coefficient at every integer. Hence $G$ has a zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Compactness of critical rescalings]\label{lem:criticalcompact}
Let $n\to\infty$ along any sequence and choose $w_n$ in a fixed annulus $\Aab$. Put $r_n=|w_n|$ and choose $j=j(n)$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:critj}
 \nu_j\leq n+r_n\sqrt n<\nu_{j+1},\qquad q=q_j.
\end{equation}
Then $\nu_j\geq n$ eventually. The entire functions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rescaling}
 G_n(v)=\frac{F_{4/3}^{(n)}(w_n\ee^{v/q})}
                  {T_j(w_n\ee^{v/q})}
\end{equation}
have a subsequence converging locally uniformly to a function \eqref{eq:gaussian} with $c_0=1$, $\kappa=(4/3)^2/r$ for some $r\in[a,b]$, and finite real $t$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:gaps}, $q\sim(4/3)n^{1/4}$ and $m_j=r_n\sqrt n+O(q)$. For each fixed integer $s$, Taylor's theorem for $x^{4/3}$ and the bounded floor errors give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:increment}
 \Delta_{n,s}:=\nu_{j+s}-\nu_j=sq+O_s(1),
 \qquad\Delta_{n,s}/q\to s.
\end{equation}
Every fixed $s$ is active eventually: there are $\asymp\sqrt n/q\to\infty$ active support steps to the left of $j$.

Set $t_n=q\,d_{n,r_n}(\nu_j)$. Formula \eqref{eq:difference}, together with $0\leq n+r_n\sqrt n-\nu_j<q$, shows $t_n=O_{a,b}(1)$. After passage to a subsequence, $r_n\to r$ and $t_n\to t$. Uniformly on each fixed number of adjacent sparse blocks, \eqref{eq:curvature} is $(1+o(1))/(r_n\sqrt n)$. Summing the full differences, for positive and negative integer increments alike, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:criticalweights}
 \Phi_{n,r_n}(\nu_{j+s})-\Phi_{n,r_n}(\nu_j)
 =s t_n-\frac{q^2}{2r_n\sqrt n}s^2+o(1)
 \longrightarrow st-\kappa s^2/2.
\end{equation}
For clarity, the discrete quadratic term before taking limits is
$-\Delta_{n,s}(\Delta_{n,s}-1)/(2r_n\sqrt n)$; replacing it by the term displayed above changes it by $o(1)$.

The coefficient multiplying $\exp(\Delta_{n,s}v/q)$ in \eqref{eq:rescaling} has modulus given by \eqref{eq:criticalweights} and phase $\exp(i\Delta_{n,s}\arg w_n)$. A diagonal subsequence makes these unit phases converge for every integer $s$, giving coefficients $c_s$ with $c_0=1$. We do not replace the integer parts in these phases by real powers.

We verify the tails needed to pass from coefficient convergence to local uniform convergence. Fix $A>0$ and $|\Rea v|\leq A$. The real part of each exponent is the logarithmic weight at radius $r_n\exp(\Rea v/q)$, normalized by the $j$th term. For a fixed sufficiently large integer $S$, \eqref{eq:criticalweights} gives, at $s=S$ and $s=-S$, an upper bound
$C_A S-cS^2$, with $c>0$ depending only on the annulus. The threshold in $n$ may depend on $S,A$. Formula \eqref{eq:difference} and local curvature likewise show that beyond the right endpoint the full slope is at most $-c'S/q$, and before the left endpoint it is at least $c'S/q$, after choosing $S$ large compared with $A$. Global monotonicity preserves these bounds all the way to the ends of the active support. Every active gap is at least a fixed positive multiple of $q$. Each further sparse step therefore loses at least $c''S$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crittail}
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \sup_{|\Rea v|\leq A}
 \sum_{|s|\geq S}|\text{the }s\text{th normalized term}|
 \leq\frac{C\exp(C_A S-cS^2)}{1-\exp(-c''S)}.
\end{equation}
Here inactive left terms are omitted. The right side tends to zero with $S$. Finite-term convergence and this tail bound prove local uniform convergence to \eqref{eq:gaussian}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the critical upper bound]
Suppose the upper bound in \eqref{eq:criticalcover} fails. There are $n_h\to\infty$ and $w_h\in\Aab$ with
$\dist(w_h,\Zer(F_{4/3}^{(n_h)}))>h n_h^{-1/4}$.
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:criticalcompact} and then Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian}. The nonzero limit $G$ has a zero. Choose a small circle around that zero on which $G$ has no zeros. Local uniform convergence and Rouch\'e's theorem give zeros $v_h$ of $G_{n_h}$ in a fixed bounded disk. Equation \eqref{eq:rescaling} yields zeros
$\zeta_h=w_h\exp(v_h/q_{j(n_h)})$ of the original derivatives, satisfying
$|\zeta_h-w_h|\leq C/q_{j(n_h)}\leq C'n_h^{-1/4}$. This contradicts the choice of $w_h$.
\end{proof}

\section{A zero-count upper bound and final deductions}\label{sec:final}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:diskcount}
For every fixed $R>0$ and $4/3\leq p<2$, the number of zeros of $F_p^{(n)}$ in $|z|\leq R$, counted with multiplicity, is $O_{R,p}(\sqrt n)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $n\geq1$ and $r>0$, Cauchy--Schwarz and the negative-binomial series give
\begin{align}
 S_n(r)&:=\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{\sqrt{(n+m)!}}{m!}r^m\notag\\
 &\leq\sqrt{n!}(1-t)^{-(n+1)/2}\exp(r^2/(2t)),\qquad 0<t<1.\label{eq:Sn}
\end{align}
Indeed, after dividing by $\sqrt{n!}$, each term is
$\sqrt{\binom{n+m}{n}/m!}\,r^m$, and the two squared sums are
$\sum_m\binom{n+m}{n}t^m=(1-t)^{-n-1}$ and $\sum_m(r^2/t)^m/m!=\exp(r^2/t)$.
Taking $t=r/(r+\sqrt n)$ and using $\log(1+x)\leq x$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:snbound}
 \log S_n(r)\leq\tfrac12\log(n!)+r\sqrt n+(r+r^2)/2.
\end{equation}
This estimate also appears in Hou \cite[Lemma~2.1]{Hou26}; its elementary derivation is included here.

Factor $F_p^{(n)}(z)=z^{m_0}g_n(z)$ with $m_0=m_{j_0}=O(Q)=o(\sqrt n)$.
For large $n$, $m_0\leq\sqrt n$ and
\[
 g_n(0)=\frac{\sqrt{(n+m_0)!}}{m_0!}\geq\sqrt{n!},
\]
since $m_0!\leq m_0^{m_0}\leq n^{m_0/2}$, with the case $m_0=0$ read separately. Choose a fixed $L>\max(1,R)$, for instance $L=2(R+1)$. On $|z|=L$,
$|g_n(z)|\leq L^{-m_0}S_n(L)\leq S_n(L)$. Jensen's formula bounds the number of zeros of $g_n$ in $|z|\leq R$ by
\[
 \frac{\log S_n(L)-\log g_n(0)}{\log(L/R)}=O_R(\sqrt n).
\]
Adding the origin multiplicity proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

The lower bound in \eqref{eq:criticalcover} follows from area. The upper bound already proved implies $h_n(\Aab)<1$ eventually. Zeros in the disk $|z|\leq b+1$ suffice to cover $\Aab$ by disks of radius $h_n(\Aab)$. There are at most $C\sqrt n$ such zeros by Lemma~\ref{lem:diskcount}. Therefore
$\pi(b^2-a^2)\leq C\sqrt n\,\pi h_n(\Aab)^2$, giving the asserted lower bound.

Finally, every nonempty open set contains a nonzero point $w$ and a disk $D(w,\varepsilon)$ contained in a fixed annulus. The upper bounds in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} tend to zero for every sufficiently large integer $n$. They place a derivative zero in that disk, proving \eqref{eq:cofinite} in both regimes. The subsequence in the critical proof is used to contradict failure of a uniform bound; the final assertion is not restricted to a subsequence.

\subsection*{Further questions}
For $p=3/2$, the sharp annular covering scale is $n^{-1/6}$; at $p=4/3$ it is $n^{-1/4}$. The endpoint is the least explicit part of the argument: the proof gives neither a circular grid nor eventual simplicity or a limiting zero measure, and I do not claim any of these there. The range $1<p<4/3$ seems to require a different analysis. I would also like an effective version of Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian}, since it would turn the compactness proof into explicit constants for the critical covering theorem.
